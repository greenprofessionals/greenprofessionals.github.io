The Arithmetic of Amendment
Sierra Leone's Constitution of Sierra Leone (Amendment) Bill, 2025 needs 100 votes to pass and, on present numbers, cannot get there without the APC. This piece sets out what the bill actually does, what the Constitution requires to enact it, and what is and is not yet confirmed about where Parliament stands.

What the Bill Actually Does

The bill is formally titled the Constitution of Sierra Leone (Amendment) Act, 2025, though it remains a bill rather than an enacted Act. It was made on 1 December 2025 by Attorney-General and Minister of Justice Alpha Sesay, who has piloted it through Parliament, and it draws on both the 2013 Justice Edmond Cowan Constitutional Review Committee Report and the more recent Tripartite Committee's eighteen consensus recommendations.

Its center of gravity is electoral governance rather than a full constitutional redesign. It would make district-based proportional representation the permanent method of electing ordinary MPs, while leaving the detailed design of that system, including district magnitude and vote thresholds, to later legislation. It would replace the current 55 percent presidential election threshold with a simple national majority combined with a requirement to reach at least 20 percent of the vote in two-thirds of districts. It would permit independent presidential candidates for the first time, subject to financial-capacity and community-support conditions still to be defined. It would fix presidential and parliamentary elections for the second Saturday of November every fifth year, impose caretaker restrictions on an outgoing president between election day and inauguration, restructure how electoral commissioners are appointed, entrench a 30 percent quota for women candidates, create a new process for deregistering parties that win no seats in two successive elections, and add explicit political-neutrality requirements for the police and armed forces.

It does not touch the two-term presidential limit in section 46, does not restructure the judiciary, and does not transfer additional constitutional powers or revenue to local government, notwithstanding broader government messaging about decentralization.

Dual Citizenship and the Diaspora

For Diaspora Readers

No, the gazetted bill does not open Parliament to dual citizens. This is worth stating plainly because it is the single provision diaspora readers are most likely to ask about, and it is easy to misread.

Section 76(1)(a) of the current Constitution disqualifies from election as an MP anyone who is a naturalized Sierra Leonean, anyone who voluntarily acquired the citizenship of another country, or anyone under a declared allegiance to another country. Clause 14 of the gazetted December 2025 bill amends section 76, but it does not repeal that disqualification. What it adds is narrower: a Sierra Leonean citizen by birth who also holds another nationality may be appointed a minister. Parliamentary eligibility itself is untouched. That creates an odd asymmetry: a qualifying dual citizen could sit in Cabinet but could not have been elected to the Parliament that confirms Cabinet appointments in the first place.

This gap is not accidental or new. In October 2021, President Bio publicly announced that his government had authorized the Attorney-General to draft a constitutional amendment allowing dual citizens to be elected as MPs, following years of advocacy led by Dr. Kandeh Kolleh Yumkella and others. The post-2023 Tripartite Committee later made a specific recommendation along similar lines: preserve a single-citizenship requirement for the presidency, but open parliamentary candidacy to dual citizens. Neither the 2021 commitment nor the Tripartite recommendation is self-executing. Both required an actual amendment to section 76(1)(a), and the gazetted bill does not make one.

The gap resurfaced on 28 July 2026, though reporting on exactly who said what is uneven and worth walking through carefully. The most clearly reported advocate for full parliamentary eligibility was APC MP Hon. Abdul Karim Kamara, who is reported as calling on Parliament to permit dual citizens to contest parliamentary elections. Fellow APC MP Hon. Aminata Sesay is separately reported as raising the diaspora's economic and developmental contribution and seeking clarification from the Attorney-General on citizenship rules. On the SLPP side, one detailed chamber account identifies Hon. Mariama Munia Zombo as agreeing with the principle, conditioned on a residence requirement before a returning candidate could stand.

Two attributions that might seem natural do not hold up under closer sourcing. The Attorney-General's own July 28 ministerial statement, as reported in the most detailed contemporaneous accounts, covers the bill's origins, proportional representation, and the women's quota, but does not include him calling for repeal of the section 76(1)(a) disqualification; the gazetted text he signed retains it. And the only report naming Hon. Wuyatta Songa in connection with this debate describes her challenging fellow MP Aminata Sesay over an alleged British passport, which reads as invoking the existing restriction rather than arguing to lift it. Neither attribution should be treated as confirmed, and the Songa attribution in particular points the opposite direction from a reform push.

The fairest current summary is that dual-citizen parliamentary eligibility was actively discussed on 28 July, with the clearest named support coming from APC and one identified SLPP MP favoring it with safeguards, while no reliable public record shows either the Attorney-General or Hon. Songa personally advocating the change that day. That leaves the broader point from President Bio's 2021 commitment and the Tripartite Committee's recommendation intact: this is a government-originated promise that has gone unfulfilled in the government's own bill, and named members from both sides are now working the gap in different ways, rather than a clean party-line dispute. No formal amendment text, mover, or seconder for a section 76 change had been publicly confirmed as of 3 August 2026.

A separate complication: reported 2024 legislation specifically excludes dual citizens from the Speakership on grounds of undivided allegiance, even as the door has been narrowly opened for ministerial appointment. That suggests Sierra Leone's reform, so far, is proceeding office by office rather than through a single coherent principle, which is itself worth watching as the bill moves through Committee Stage.

An amendment confined to section 76 would not, on its own, trigger the referendum requirement discussed above, since section 76 is not among the provisions entrenched by section 108(3). But if sections 85 and 87 remain in the final package, the bill as a whole may still require a referendum regardless of what happens with section 76.

Status as of 3 August 2026: no publicly available Committee Stage amendment paper, division list, or revised bill text has been located confirming that section 76(1)(a) has actually been amended. The defensible statement is that MPs proposed extending parliamentary eligibility to dual citizens and that this proposal has real institutional backing, not that the change has been made. Readers should treat any claim that "the bill now allows dual citizens to become MPs" as premature until that text is confirmed.

The 100-Vote Problem

Section 108 of the Constitution requires the support of not less than two-thirds of all Members of Parliament at both Second and Third Reading before a constitutional amendment can proceed. With 149 seats in the current Parliament, two-thirds is not a rounded figure; the threshold is at least 100 affirmative votes.

This is the single fact that should organize how the bill's prospects are read. It is not primarily a question of whether the government wants the bill to pass, or whether its provisions are individually popular; it is a question of whether the SLPP can secure five additional votes from a 54-member opposition bloc that has, at various points in the process, both welcomed reform in principle and objected to specific mechanics, timing, and process. The Deputy Speaker of Parliament has himself said publicly that he expects the APC to consider a walkout or other protest action at the point of a vote, which is itself a signal of how contested the numbers are.

The Referendum Trapdoor

A second, less widely understood threshold sits behind the first. Section 108(3) entrenches certain constitutional provisions, including sections 85 and 87, which the bill would amend to fix Parliament's term to the date of the next general election and cap emergency extensions. Because those sections are entrenched, any bill that alters them cannot take effect on a parliamentary vote alone; it must also be approved at a national referendum, and not on the ordinary referendum test of a simple majority. The Constitution requires affirmative votes equal to at least half of all persons entitled to vote, in addition to at least two-thirds of the valid votes actually cast.

The practical implication is that low turnout does not make a referendum easier to pass, as it might for an ordinary majority vote; it makes this particular referendum harder, because half of the entire electorate, voting or not, must vote in favor. Parliament's only route around this second threshold is to remove or sever the amendments to sections 85 and 87 before Third Reading, or to redesign the bill so that entrenched provisions are not touched.

What Is Actually Confirmed

Public reporting on the Second Reading debate of 28 to 29 July 2026 is not fully consistent. Some accounts describe the marathon overnight session as following the bill's First Reading; others describe the same Attorney-General as having piloted the bill through its Second and Third Readings in that period. The Deputy Speaker of Parliament's public remarks, made after that session, refer to an anticipated future vote rather than a completed one, and debate was reported adjourned to Friday, 31 July 2026, for continuation. No official division list, certified vote count, or Votes and Proceedings record confirming a completed Second Reading passage was located.

Drafting Defects Worth Watching

Independent of the political questions, several drafting issues in the gazetted text create legal uncertainty that Committee Stage would need to resolve. A clause meant to cap wartime extensions of the presidential term refers, apparently in error, to the "term of Parliament" rather than the presidency. Separate clauses both state that loss of party membership alone cannot remove a sitting president or vice president, and permit a two-thirds parliamentary vote to remove either following resignation or expulsion from their party, a tension that would need explicit reconciliation. The consequential amendment to the Public Elections Act contains a placeholder requiring action "within days" without specifying a number. Independent presidential candidates would need to show undefined "sufficient financial capacity," a standard that has already drawn criticism from Sierra Leone's Human Rights Commission as vague and potentially discriminatory on the basis of wealth.

Geography of Attention

The bill's shift to permanent proportional representation ties parliamentary seat allocation to the most recent decennial census, and bars census data published within 24 months of an election from being used for that election. That detail matters for districts like Kailahun, which has historically returned strong SLPP majorities while remaining comparatively under-resourced after election cycles end. A seat-allocation formula anchored to census timing, rather than to sustained investment or population growth trends, does nothing on its own to correct that pattern of high loyalty paired with post-election neglect. If proportional representation is meant to broaden inclusion, the districts most consistently overlooked once the votes are counted deserve the same scrutiny in implementing legislation that the presidential threshold and independent-candidacy provisions are receiving now.

Open Questions

Several matters remain unresolved and worth tracking as the bill moves, if it moves, through Committee Stage: whether sections 85 and 87 survive to Third Reading and trigger the referendum requirement; what statutory threshold and district magnitude will govern the new proportional system; whether independent candidates will be permitted to contest parliamentary as well as presidential seats; whether section 76(1)(a) will be amended to extend parliamentary eligibility to dual citizens, as MPs proposed on 28 July, or whether the bill will pass with only the narrower ministerial-appointment provision intact; how caretaker restrictions on "major appointments" and "major contracts" will be defined and enforced; and whether Parliament will publish a certified division list establishing that the constitutionally required supermajority was actually achieved at Second Reading, whenever that vote is confirmed to have occurred.
